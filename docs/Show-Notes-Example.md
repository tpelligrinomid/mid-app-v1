## Episode Summary

In this episode of Payments on Tap, host Elyssa Morgan sits down with Angela Murphy, Vice President of Marketing and Solutions at Pidgin and widely known across the industry as Payments Elsa. They explore what it takes to build visibility, credibility, and influence in an industry that runs on assumptions and alphabet soup.

Angela shares how she moved from a PhD in Rhetoric to a career in payments — not through a traditional path, but through coffee meetings, flashcards, and a willingness to start from zero. She explains why the stories told about how money moves have never been good enough, and how that gap became her opening.

The conversation turns to the difference between authority and influence. Angela argues that titles and tenure mean little without the ability to listen, understand, and move people with information. She makes the case that confidence is not something you're born with — it's something you anchor to what you already know and carry into unfamiliar rooms.

For leaders and rising professionals in payments and fintech, the takeaway is clear: start small, ask the question, and trust the process.

## Key Insights

### Payments needs better storytellers, not just better technology

Angela didn't enter payments through banking or financial services. She came from academia with a PhD in Rhetoric and a habit of looking for problems worth solving. When she arrived, she noticed the industry was full of knowledge but thin on clarity. The stories being told about how money moves weren't reaching the people who needed them most. That became her lane. She chose to focus on making payments and financial technology more accessible — not by simplifying the work, but by improving how it's communicated. For executives, the lesson is direct: the ability to translate complexity into clear language is a strategic advantage. Teams that can explain what they do — to boards, partners, and customers — hold a different kind of power than teams that just execute.

### Influence carries further than authority

Angela draws a sharp line between authority and influence. Authority says, "It's this way because I said so." Influence says, "Here's what I understand, here's what I've learned, and here's why it matters." She argues that the people treated as authorities in payments often lack the perspective to move the industry forward. Real progress comes from people who listen, learn, and bring others into the conversation. For leaders at financial institutions and fintechs, this reframes how to think about internal culture, client relationships, and industry presence. The strongest voices in a room are rarely the loudest. They're the ones others choose to follow.

### Starting from zero is a valid strategy

Angela started her payments career with no industry background, no network, and no shorthand for the acronyms. She studied with color-coded flashcards, stayed late, and asked every question she could. That willingness to begin at the bottom — and to treat the gap in her knowledge as fuel — became the foundation for everything that followed. This matters for organizations thinking about talent pipelines. The industry doesn't teach itself in a classroom. You don't go to college for payments. That means the best hires may come from unexpected places, and the best development plans start with permission to learn in the open.

## Episode Highlights

### Coffee meetings built the career map

**Timestamp: \~00:02:30**

Before Angela ever entered payments, she took a simple approach to figuring out her next move. She had coffee with anyone who would meet her, handed them her resume, and asked one question: "If you had this resume, how would you talk about your skillset?" That question — direct, humble, and strategic — helped her identify the thread that connected her academic skills to a professional identity. The result: "I work at the intersection of strategy and insight." For professionals in transition, this is a repeatable model. Don't wait for the industry to find you. Ask better questions and let other people's experience help you see what you already bring.

"I just told them my story. I said that I really cared, that I wanted to try, and if they would give me a chance that I would do my best."

### A champion changed the trajectory

**Timestamp: \~00:11:08**

Angela credits Martha Salinas as the person who opened doors she never would have reached on her own. Martha brought her into meetings, gave her space to take notes, and then took the time to answer every question afterward. That investment created a model Angela now carries into her own mentoring. The point for leaders: sponsorship is not a title. It's a behavior. Bringing someone into a room and then helping them understand what happened in it is one of the highest-impact things a leader can do.

"She brought me into several rooms that I would never have been in otherwise."

### The alphabet soup problem is a leadership problem

**Timestamp: \~00:08:02**

Angela describes the payments industry as a world filled with assumptions — acronyms, phraseology, and tribal knowledge that insiders take for granted. She saw that gap early and recognized it as an opportunity. If so many people assume everyone else understands the language, there's a large group that's been left behind. For institutions, this is more than a communications issue. It's a talent issue, an onboarding issue, and a customer experience issue. The organizations that make payments language accessible will build stronger teams and better products.

"We all just take for granted that money goes from here to here. And we don't think about why and we don't think about impact."

### Authority without influence stalls progress

**Timestamp: \~00:26:31**

Angela argues that authority alone doesn't move an industry forward. She's seen people lean on tenure and titles to shut down conversations rather than open them. Her view: if you can influence decisions through understanding and information, that carries more weight than any credential. This matters inside organizations where decision-making power often sits with the longest-tenured voice. If that voice isn't also the most informed and most curious, the institution moves slower than the market around it.

"Your ability to understand others and to influence others is what provides authority in those situations."
