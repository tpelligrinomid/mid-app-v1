# The Retainer Model, Simplified

*Scope note: this covers retainers only. Projects — website builds, brand work, one-time engagements — are milestone-fee or one-time point purchases and sit outside this model.*

---

## The problem in one paragraph

We sell the same thing to everyone. A $4,000 client and a $12,000 client get access to everything we do, the same weekly meetings, and the same implied accountability for pipeline. Nothing in our system tells anyone what an engagement is supposed to look like, so strategists improvise it account by account and sales negotiates it deal by deal. That's why margins are compressed, why scope creeps, and why clients at modest retainers hold us accountable for outcomes we don't control.

Three fixes.

---

## Fix 1 — Programs and tiers

### Service categories

The work itself. Every task tags to one. This is what shows in the client portal.

**Strategy & account management** runs under every engagement and is never sold on its own.

**Five categories run across programs — Content, Design, Analytics & reporting, and (in two of three) Development and Marketing operations.** That is deliberate, and it is the single most important thing to convey in a sales conversation.

A client at $6,000 is not buying "some blog posts." They are buying a writer, a designer, a developer, a marketing ops person, an analyst, a paid media specialist, and a strategist — coordinated, under one account, with one point of contact. Hiring those roles separately means seven vendor relationships or seven salaries. **That is the value story, and until now it has been invisible because everything was buried inside a monthly fee.**

The portal makes it visible. A Perform client running Authority and Reach sees nine or ten bars every month. They can count the skill sets.

**Content is in all three programs.** You cannot run paid without something worth promoting, and you cannot run outbound without a landing page and an offer behind it. What differs between programs is the volume and the type, not whether content is there:

| Program | What the content is | Rough share of program points |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Authority** | Net-new thought leadership, search-driven pieces, premium assets. Content *is* the program. | \~50% |
| **Reach** | Offers, ad copy, landing page copy, nurture sequences. Conversion-focused, lower volume. | \~30% |
| **Pursuit** | Sequence copy, account-relevant one-pagers, tailored offers. | \~25% |

That's a useful check for strategists. An Authority engagement running 20% content is wrong. A Reach engagement running 60% content is also wrong — it means nothing is getting distributed.

**And it's the argument for two programs.** Authority produces the material; Reach and Pursuit put it in front of people. A client buying only Reach is distributing thin assets to the right audience. A client buying only Authority is making excellent material that reaches whoever stumbles across it. Neither is a complete motion, which is why Perform is where most clients should land.

**What each program is for:**

| Program | The question it answers |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Authority** | Be the company your market trusts before they're ready to buy. |
| **Reach** | Get in front of the right buyers when you don't know their names yet. |
| **Pursuit** | Win the specific accounts you've decided you want. |

**On the shared categories.** Content in Authority is thought leadership and search-driven material for an unnamed market. Content in Reach is offers, ad copy, and landing page copy built to convert. Content in Pursuit is account-relevant one-pagers and sequence copy for a named list. Same skill, same category, same tagging — different target. Design, Development, and Marketing operations work the same way: a landing page needs all three regardless of whether paid traffic or a named account list is pointed at it.

This matters for one practical reason: **a category showing up in a client's portal doesn't tell you which program it served.** The program does. Don't let anyone try to infer program from category.

### What each category covers

Every task in the Process Library gets one of these. Where a task could plausibly fit two, the tiebreaker is *what skill did this require*, not *what was it for*.

**Strategy & account management** Roadmapping, 90-day planning, campaign strategy, client communication, status calls, QBR prep, analysis and recommendations, internal coordination. *Not:* producing anything. If a deliverable came out of it, tag the deliverable's category.

**Content** Writing and editing in any format — blog posts, thought leadership, whitepapers, case studies, email copy, ad copy, sequence copy, landing page copy, sales enablement, scripts. Also keyword research, content planning, SEO optimization of existing pieces, technical SEO, schema markup, and GEO work. *Not:* the design or build of the thing the copy lives in.

**Design** Visual work in any format — ad creative, landing page and web design, social graphics, premium asset layout, infographics, brand application, presentation design. *Not:* the copy inside it. *Not:* the front-end build that follows.

**Development** Front-end build, page and template implementation, integrations, custom functionality, site maintenance, page speed and technical fixes. *Not:* the design that preceded it. *Not:* a full website rebuild — that's a project, outside the retainer model.

**Marketing operations** CRM administration, marketing automation configuration, workflow and lifecycle stage build, lead scoring and routing, list and data hygiene, field and property management, system-to-system integrations, tracking and tag implementation. *Not:* the reports and dashboards built on top of it. That's Analytics & reporting. *Not:* the analysis of what the data says. That's Strategy.

**Analytics & reporting** Reporting infrastructure and dashboard build, data source and platform integrations, custom report design, attribution configuration, conversion and event tracking design, data QA, recurring report production. *Not:* configuring the CRM the data comes from. That's Marketing operations. *Not:* the strategic interpretation and recommendations. That's Strategy.

*This is often the same person as Marketing operations, and that's fine — the categories describe the work, not the org chart. Keeping them separate matters because reporting is one of the few things every client gets, at every tier, and it should be visible as its own line rather than absorbed into ops.*

**Organic social** Channel strategy, post creation and scheduling, community management, employee advocacy programs, social listening. *Not:* paid amplification of a post. That's Paid media.

**Podcast & video** Show strategy, guest booking, recording coordination, editing, production, show notes, clip and repurposing work. *Not:* the blog post written from an episode. That's Content.

**Digital PR** *(subcontracted — billed separately, not points)* Media relations, link building, contributed article placement, podcast guesting, awards and speaking submissions.

**Paid media** Channel strategy, campaign build, audience and list targeting, bid and budget management, optimization, campaign reporting. Includes account-based paid against a named list. *Not:* the ad creative itself — that's Design. *Not:* ad copy — that's Content. *Not:* ad spend, which is client-direct.

**Email & nurture** Lifecycle and nurture strategy, sequence build in the marketing platform, list segmentation, deliverability on owned domains, newsletter operations. *Not:* cold outreach on alternative domains. That's Outbound. *Not:* the copy in the emails — that's Content. *Not:* the underlying automation and CRM configuration — that's Marketing operations.

**Outbound** Cold outreach on alternative domains. Domain and inbox provisioning, DNS authentication, warming, list building and enrichment, signal research, sequence configuration, deliverability monitoring and inbox rotation, reply triage and routing. *Not:* nurture to an owned list. That's Email & nurture. *Not:* the copy itself, which is Content.

**A note on why Outbound is separate from Email & nurture.** They share a medium and almost nothing else. Nurture works an owned list on the primary domain and the skill is lifecycle logic. Outbound is cold, on burner domains, and the skill is deliverability engineering. Merging them hides the fact that we're running sending infrastructure on a client's behalf — with real domain and reputation risk attached.

**A note on landing pages.** A landing page is not one category. The copy is Content, the layout is Design, the build is Development, and the tracking is Marketing operations. That is not bureaucracy — it is precisely the point. Four skill sets on one deliverable is the value story, and the portal should show it.

### Tiers

| Tier | Investment | Programs | We're accountable for |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Execute** | $4,000–5,900 | One | Delivery — what shipped, on time, to standard |
| **Perform** | $6,000–11,900 | Two | Delivery plus the leading indicators we control |
| **Grow** | $12,000+ | All three | The same, plus we own the plan and the measurement |

|  | Execute | Perform | Grow |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Points/mo | 40–59 | 60–119 | 120+ |
| Strategy ownership | Client owns; we execute | Shared — we recommend, they decide | We own the plan |
| Reported | Delivery log, channel basics | Traffic, conversion rate, CPL, form fills, self-reported source | Full funnel view, cohort analysis, closed-loop where data allows |
| Cadence | Monthly call | Biweekly \+ 90-day plan | Weekly \+ QBR |
| Strategist | Pooled team \+ account director | Dedicated strategist \+ account director | Dedicated \+ exec sponsor |
| Turnaround | 5–7 days | 3–5 days | 48 hours |

**Buy a program, get the categories inside it.** That's the whole rule. A client asking for paid media is asking for Reach or Pursuit.

**Why the tiers land where they do.** Every account burns roughly 12 points a month on strategy and account management before anything ships. A program needs about 45–50 points to run its categories properly. So one program is \~60 points, two is \~110, three is \~155. The tiers are arithmetic, not policy.

**Two rules that keep this honest:**

**Pursuit is not available at Execute.** Outbound needs domains, inbox warming, and a four-to-six week ramp before anything sends, plus a landing page and content behind it. At 40–59 points that's infrastructure with nothing behind it — which is exactly the situation that has been going badly. Pursuit starts at Perform, paired with Authority or Reach.

**We do not take accountability for pipeline. At any tier. Ever.**

This is a deliberate position, not a hedge, and everyone needs to be able to explain it:

Pipeline requires us to accept accountability for a number the client controls entirely. Opportunity value is self-reported. Stage definitions are theirs. Whether a deal is logged at all depends on their rep's discipline. And most of our clients don't hold *themselves* accountable for pipeline — their marketing lead doesn't carry a pipeline number and their sales team has no documented SLA. Accepting an orphaned metric nobody internally owns isn't rigor.

The low-deal-count problem makes it worse. A client closing three to five deals a year has pipeline that is statistical noise. One enterprise deal slipping a quarter swamps everything we did.

**What we say instead:**

> "We don't take accountability for pipeline, and I'd be skeptical of anyone who does. Pipeline depends on your sales follow-up, your close rate, how your reps stage deals, and whether the opportunity gets logged at all — none of which we control or can verify. What we do is model it from your numbers so you know what to expect, and then we're accountable for every input on our side of the chain. When something underperforms, we can show you which link broke."

Grow's value is scope, strategic ownership, measurement infrastructure, and speed — not outcome liability.

*Note on the Grow floor: three programs at \~155 points is $15,500, not $12,000. Either we move the floor up or Grow clients run three programs somewhat thin. Worth deciding deliberately — see decisions.*

### The ramp period

**We are not accountable for lead volume in month one.** Third-party research puts the B2B buying journey at nine to ten months from first exposure to closed deal, with roughly three-quarters of it happening anonymously before a buyer contacts anyone. That means about six months of pre-lead activity before a hand goes up.

Every SOW states a ramp period derived from the client's own numbers — deal size, category presence, and where they can supply it, how long their recent customers knew about them before reaching out.

| Period | What we hold ourselves to |
| :---- | :---- |
| Months 1–2 | Delivery volume, baseline established, tracking live |
| Months 2–4 | Traffic, engagement, content velocity, reach |
| Months 4–6 | Conversion rate, form fills, self-reported source volume |
| Month 6+ | Full leading-indicator set against the baseline |

**Targets are set at the end of the ramp, against observed baselines** — not at signature, against guesses.

**Build in a mid-ramp checkpoint at month three.** Six months is a long time to be unfalsifiable. If traffic and engagement aren't moving by month three, that's a real problem regardless of where we are in the buying cycle. Something has to be measurable early or the ramp becomes a place to hide.

The client-facing benchmark document (*What It Actually Takes*) carries the third-party research and belongs in every AGE.

### Reporting depth scales with the tier

Analytics & reporting is in every program and every tier, but what it produces is not the same thing. **What we're accountable for determines what we build.**

| Tier | What we build | Roughly |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Execute** | Delivery log and standard channel reporting. What shipped, what it did. | 3–5 pts/mo |
| **Perform** | Custom dashboard, multi-source integration, funnel and conversion reporting, campaign attribution. | 8–12 pts/mo |
| **Grow** | Full-funnel visibility, CRM-to-marketing integration, cohort and lifecycle views, closed-loop reporting where the client's data supports it. | 15–20 pts/mo |

This is a concrete answer to "what do I get for more money" that isn't just volume. Building and running the measurement is real, ongoing work, and it's what makes the undercount smaller.

It also gives sales something to point at when a Perform client asks for deeper reporting: that's a Grow-level build, here's what it takes.

### Reporting the number honestly

Because most of the journey is invisible, tracked conversions capture a fraction of reality. Two rules:

**Self-reported source is the primary instrument.** "How did you hear about us?" as open text on the form and again in the first sales call. Crude, unfashionable, and the only thing that catches dark social. **This is a client obligation in the SOW**, not a suggestion — it only works if their sales team asks consistently.

**Report total volume against baseline, not "leads we sourced."** Claiming every lead invites a fight we'll eventually lose. Showing the trend doesn't:

> Baseline before engagement: 12/month. Current: 31/month. Tracked to our channels: 14\. Self-reported to our channels: 9\. Unattributed: 8\.

The unattributed bucket is visible rather than contested. We're not claiming those eight — we're showing that total volume moved and that untracked leads grew alongside tracked ones, which is what happens when marketing works.

**Set the baseline in month one, in writing.** Without it, every future conversation is about whether the number is good rather than whether it moved.

**Say the undercount up front, at kickoff:**

> "Tracked conversions will undercount by a third to half. That's true for everyone. We'll use self-reported source as the primary instrument and treat tracked numbers as a floor. If we don't agree on that now, in nine months you'll be looking at a dashboard showing twelve leads while you close deals that aren't in it."

Said in month one, that's competence. Said when results are questioned, it's an excuse.

## Fix 2 — Technology comes out of the fee

Thirty-two of our forty active retainers carry technology inside the monthly fee. That's $35,184/mo — 14% of revenue — invisible to the client and to us.

Sedra pays $5,000/mo and carries $2,420 of platform. So we deliver 75 points of labor for $2,580, and nobody has ever said that out loud.

**Every retainer with technology gets a separate line item.** Not buried, not absorbed.

**How to transition without documenting a discount.** If we separate the line and hold the total constant, Sedra's invoice reads $2,580 for 75 points — $34 a point against our $100 rack rate, in writing, forever. Don't do that.

Separate technology **and** reset the point allotment to tier rate in the same motion. Sedra becomes Perform: $6,000 for 60 points, plus $2,420 platform billed separately. One conversation, one change, one story — "we've restructured how we scope and bill."

## Fix 3 — A points policy that caps our exposure

Points accrue on invoice, not on delivery. That's correct and it shouldn't change — the client bought capacity for the month and the retainer's value is that revenue is decoupled from throughput. Pausing accrual when a client stalls would mean holding capacity while earning nothing, which is worse than the problem.

**The fix is what happens to unused points, not how they accrue.**

WSB is the case study. Approval delays on their side meant we couldn't implement, points kept accruing, and the balance reached 414 — eight months of work owed. They then stepped down from $20,000 to $5,000/mo with the full balance intact. We'll be delivering against that backlog for most of a year at a fifth of the revenue that funded it.

**Three rules, in the MSA:**

1. **Rollover caps at 150% of monthly allotment.** Points above the cap expire each period unless the parties agree in writing to a documented catch-up schedule. Under this rule WSB's balance surfaces as a conversation at month four, not a crisis at month twenty.  
     
2. **Continuous visibility, with a 30-day dispute window.** The client can see their balance, delivery history, and work in progress at all times. Discrepancies must be raised within 30 days of the billing period. Balances not disputed are deemed accurate. Without this, a client can contest a year of delivery at renewal and we're reconstructing history under pressure.  
     
3. **Points expire on termination.** Already in the current MSA and it's right. What needs adding is that points continue to accrue and remain deliverable through the effective termination date, then expire — otherwise a notice period is ambiguous.

**Three operational fixes matter more than the contract language**, and they can start immediately with existing clients:

**Default-forward approvals.** Not "please approve this" but "we're proceeding Friday unless you tell us otherwise." Silence becomes consent. Most delays aren't refusals — they're a busy person who hasn't gotten to it.

**A pre-approved backlog on every account.** Evergreen work that never needs sign-off. A blocked strategist redirects instead of idling. Points get consumed, the client gets value, the deficit doesn't build. This belongs in every SOW.

**Consolidated feedback, two rounds.** Most revision pain isn't the number of rounds — it's three stakeholders sending contradictory notes across a week. One consolidated response per round forces them to resolve their own disagreements before it becomes our problem. Round three is a signal, not a task: the account director joins and the conversation changes from "here's another version" to "we've done two rounds and we're not converging — what's actually blocking this?"

**On bonus points.** The WSB SOW granted 130 points at the outset plus 15/month ongoing. Over the initial 180-day term that's roughly 1,300 points issued against 1,200 purchased. Bonus points create a delivery obligation with no revenue attached and they never expire while the engagement is active. **If they're a closing concession, they should be a discount on the fee, not free capacity we owe forever.** Any bonus points that survive should carry an expiration or a delivery schedule.

## Fix 4 — Write the constraints down before month one

One page, signed at kickoff, listing what will limit results and who owns each item.

> **Positioning** — Differentiation against \[competitor\] is unclear in current messaging. Owner: client. If unresolved: higher CPL, lower sales-accepted rate.  
>   
> **Sales follow-up** — Median time-to-first-touch is 31 hours. Owner: client. If unresolved: 40–60% of leads go cold before contact.  
>   
> **Attribution** — No closed-loop tracking. Owner: shared. If unresolved: we cannot report pipeline contribution.

Reviewed at every QBR, first item on the agenda.

**The rule that makes it work: if a constraint isn't on the page before month one, we don't raise it in month nine.** Flagged up front, it's rigor. Raised when results are questioned, it's an excuse — and clients know the difference instantly.

This is the entire answer to getting blamed for things we don't control. It costs an hour to produce.

## What this means for each group

### For clients

Published:

> **Authority** — Be the company your market trusts before they're ready to buy. **Reach** — Get in front of the right buyers when you don't know their names yet. **Pursuit** — Win the specific accounts you've decided you want.  
>   
> **Execute — from $4,000/mo.** One program. You own the plan, we execute against it. Pooled team, account director, monthly strategy call, 5–7 day turnaround. We're accountable for delivery.  
>   
> **Perform — from $6,000/mo.** Two programs. We bring the recommendations, you make the calls. Dedicated strategist, 90-day planning, 3–5 day turnaround. We're accountable for the metrics we control — traffic, conversion rate, cost per lead.  
>   
> **Grow — from $12,000/mo.** All three. We own the plan and build the measurement. Weekly cadence, 48-hour turnaround, executive sponsor.

And the line about what we don't do:

> *We don't promise a number of leads. We model one from your data, show every assumption, and hold ourselves to every input on our side of the chain.*

Plus the line that does the most work:

> *We price in points. Every engagement carries about 12 points a month of strategy, reporting, and account management before any work ships — which is why a program needs 45–50 points to run properly. We'd rather tell you that up front than take the money and under-deliver.*

**The portal shows every service category, every month.** A Perform client running Authority and Reach sees content, design, development, marketing operations, analytics and reporting, paid media, email, organic social, and strategy and account management — as separate lines with real point counts. That's how they see the range of skill sets they're actually buying, and it's how they notice when a category is running at 2 points and ask the question themselves.

### For sales

**Lead with the skill sets, not the deliverables.** This is the thing we have never said out loud and it is our strongest argument at every price point.

> "At $6,000 a month you're not hiring a content agency. You're getting a writer, a designer, a developer, a marketing ops person, an analyst, a paid media specialist, and a strategist — working together, on one plan, with one person you call. Hiring those roles yourself is seven salaries. Hiring them as freelancers is seven relationships you manage.  
>   
> Every month you'll see exactly how those points were spent across each of them. Nobody else in this category shows you that."

Then show the portal view. A prospect looking at eight or nine categories understands what they're buying in a way no capabilities deck achieves.

**The AGE ends with one sentence.** Not a menu — a determination:

> "Your roadmap requires $9,000 a month. That's Perform — two programs. We'll bring you the plan and the recommendations, you make the calls, and we're accountable for conversion rate, cost per lead, and delivery. Owning your strategy outright starts at $12,000."

**Stop letting clients anchor.** They say "we have $6,000" and we assemble a plan around it. Instead: the roadmap names the number, and the tiers show them where it lands.

**The qualification question that matters most:** *"Who owns marketing strategy on your side?"*

If the answer is a name with authority — they can be Execute or Perform. If the answer is "we were hoping you would," that's Grow, because owning strategy for a mid-market B2B tech company means understanding their category as well as they do. That's 20+ points a month before anything ships. **It will disqualify more prospects than any other change in this model, and it should.**

For Perform clients, the SOW requires a **named client-side decision owner** with approval authority. Perform doesn't work if nobody decides. Watch for the drift where a client nominally owns decisions but rubber-stamps everything and then holds us accountable for the outcome — that's Grow accountability at Perform pricing, arrived at by default. The decision log is the defense.

**On the lead-guarantee objection.** We don't refuse to talk about leads — we compute them instead of promising them. Ask for three numbers: site conversion rate, sales-accepted rate, close rate. Build the model on the call. Then:

> "The agency that promised you 50 leads — did they ask for your conversion rate before saying it?"

Most prospects can't provide those numbers, which is the answer. When they can't: *"Neither of us can forecast this yet. Let's spend 30 days building the measurement."*

**Discount rule.** 0% under 60 points. 5% at 60+. 10% at 100+. 15% at 150+. Six-month minimum term. Strategists can't discount. Sales to 5%. Anything more needs both partners.

### For strategists

**Every task tags to a service category.** That's the only new tracking requirement. Tag the category, not the program — the engagement already tells you which programs are running.

**Rough allocation targets:**

| Tier | Strategy & AM | Program work |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Execute | 30% | 70% |
| Perform | 20% | 80% |
| Grow | 12% | 88% |

Within program work, the categories should roughly reflect what the program is for. An Authority engagement running 90% Content & SEO and nothing else isn't wrong, but it's worth a conversation.

**Two numbers instead of one.** *Utilization* — did we deliver the points? That's what we measure today. *Composition* — did we deliver them in roughly the right shape? New, reviewed at the 90-day cycle, not monthly.

Composition is a diagnostic, not a compliance metric. Drift usually means the plan was wrong, the client is asking for something outside the program, or the constraint changed. All three are worth surfacing. None are the strategist's fault.

**When a client wants something outside their programs**, the answer isn't no. It's: *"That's Pursuit, which is a second program — about 60 more points. You're at 60\. Here's what it would take."*

## Rollout

1. **Tag the Process Library** — every task to a service category. This blocks everything else. Two weeks.  
2. **Pilot on 3–4 accounts** — map current allocation against program and tier. If the targets are wrong, find out on four accounts, not forty.  
3. **Sales enablement** — programs, tiers, AGE output language, discount rules.  
4. **New logos on the model immediately.** No legacy pricing.  
5. **The nine worst-margin retainers get renegotiated now**, independent of rollout. Lead with technology separation — least confrontational entry point.  
6. **Everyone else converts at renewal.** Scope grandfathered one cycle, technology separated regardless.

**Delivery goes before sales.** If sales starts selling programs before strategists have categories and targets, we'll have sold engagements nobody knows how to compose — worse than today.

## What isn't changing

A point is still a point. The menu is unchanged. Rates are unchanged. Tracking is unchanged.

What changes is that points now have a shape, and the program tells you the shape. Most of the team has been improvising this anyway — programs just make it explicit so nobody invents it from a blank page every quarter.

## What goes in the MSA vs. the SOW

The MSA holds the rules. The SOW holds the numbers. If a SOW is restating a rule, that rule belongs in the MSA — and if it's overriding one, that should be a deliberate exception with a documented reason, not a template default.

| MSA — rarely changes | SOW — per engagement |
| :---- | :---- |
| Points policy: rollover cap, expiration, termination treatment | Tier, programs, monthly allotment |
| Portal visibility \+ 30-day dispute window | Term length and notice period |
| Approval windows and defaults | Fee and payment schedule |
| Revision round defaults, consolidated feedback | Technology line items |
| Liaison authority requirement | Named Liaison and escalation contact |
| Technology-doesn't-count-toward-tier rule | Ramp period, derived from their numbers |
| No-performance-guarantee position | Constraints register (Exhibit A) |
|  | Pre-approved backlog (Exhibit B) |

**Watch for SOW drift.** MSA Section 24 makes SOW terms govern over the MSA. Every SOW that restates points terms creates an exception to whatever we standardize — the WSB amendment already does this. SOWs should *reference* the MSA policy, not restate it.

## Decisions needed

1. **Tier boundaries** — $4,000 / $6,000 / $12,000, and one / two / all three programs.  
2. **Does the Grow floor move to $15,000?** Three programs at \~155 points says yes. $12,000 is a rounder number but funds three programs thin.  
3. **Do we publish the tiers**, or use them internally? Publishing means current clients can compare — 20 of our 40 retainers sit below $6,000.  
4. **Is Pursuit-at-Perform-only the right restriction**, or should it be available at Execute with a stated minimum inside the program?  
5. **Digital PR** — published as available-billed-separately, or held off the page until we own the capability?  
6. **Discount authority** — is 5% the right cap for sales?  
7. **Who owns the category taxonomy** across the portfolio? All agencies share the delivery pool, so it has to be consistent. Same person should own the tiebreaker rulings when a task could fit two categories.  
8. **Is the Content category too broad?** It absorbs all writing plus SEO. That's simple to explain but it will be the biggest bar in most portals, which may undersell the range of work. Splitting SEO out is the obvious alternative.  
9. **The nine worst retainers** — full reset at renewal, or technology separation now and pricing next cycle?  
10. **Does the AGE get repriced?** It does the diagnostic work that determines everything above, then hands off to our least profitable retainers.  
11. **Rollover cap at 150% — right number?** And how do we handle existing balances: grandfather, wind-down schedule, or negotiate?  
12. **WSB's 414 points.** Deliver against it at $5,000/mo for eight months, negotiate a partial write-down given the approval delays that caused it, or convert some to a defined project? Resolve before the first 90-day mark, not after.  
13. **Do bonus points survive at all?** If they're a closing concession, a fee discount is cleaner. If they stay, they need an expiration or a delivery schedule.  
14. **Pipeline is off the table — is everyone aligned?** This is a real strategic position and sales will feel it in competitive deals. It needs partner-level agreement, not a memo mention.

---

## One caution

Every underperforming agency says "attribution is broken and their positioning was weak." It's both a legitimate analysis and the most common alibi in our industry.

The only thing separating us is timing. We name the constraints before we're paid, commit hard to what we control, and treat a missed forecast as seriously as anyone treats a missed guarantee. Do that and this holds. Introduce the constraints when results are questioned and we're just another agency with a good excuse.

# How We Price — Positioning and Copy

**Internal reference for the pricing page, sales deck, and proposal language.**

Everything below is client-facing copy or the reasoning behind it. Where language appears in a quote block, it's meant to be used close to verbatim — the phrasing has been worked on and small changes tend to weaken it.

---

## The positioning in one line

**You're not hiring an agency. You're hiring a marketing team.**

That's the whole argument. Every price point is defensible once a buyer understands they're funding seven or eight skill sets under one account, and indefensible if they think they're buying blog posts.

The pricing page exists to make that visible before anyone gets on a call.

---

## Why publish pricing at all

Most agencies in this category don't. Publishing does three things for us:

**It filters.** A prospect who reads "from $4,000/mo" and books a call has already accepted the range. We stop spending discovery calls on people who were never going to buy.

**It reframes the objection.** When floors and their reasoning are public, a price objection becomes a scope conversation — "here's what fits at that level" instead of "let me see what I can do."

**It's differentiating in itself.** In a category where everyone says "contact us for pricing," publishing the arithmetic reads as confidence.

The tradeoff: current clients can compare. Two-thirds of our book sits below $6,000, and some are receiving more than their tier would now include. That's handled by getting ahead of it — personal contact with the widest-gap accounts before the page goes live.

# The Pricing Page

## Section 1 — Opening

> ## Marketing built for teams that don't have one

> Most of our clients have one or two people carrying all of marketing. They don't need another vendor to manage. They need a team.  
>   
> When you work with us, you get a strategist, a writer, a designer, a developer, a marketing ops specialist, an analyst, and channel specialists — working from one plan, reporting to one person you call.  
>   
> Here's exactly what that costs and how it works.

*The specificity of the role list is doing the work. "Full-service agency" means nothing. Seven named roles means something.*

---

## Section 2 — How points work

> ## We price in points, not hours

> A point is a unit of work. An SEO blog post is 10 points. A thought leadership piece is 12\. A marketing roadmap is 40\. You buy a monthly allotment and we spend it on whatever mix makes sense that quarter.  
>   
> **Why not hours?** Because you shouldn't pay more when something takes us longer. Points price the outcome, not our efficiency.  
>   
> **What you'll see:** every point we spend is tagged to a service category and shows up in your portal. At the end of each month you can see exactly how much went to content, design, development, paid media, and everything else. Most agencies show you a list of deliverables. We show you where the work actually went.

*The portal view is the single strongest asset in the sales process. Reference it here and demo it on the call.*

---

## Section 3 — The overhead math

> ## The part most agencies don't tell you

> Every engagement carries about 12 points a month of strategy, planning, reporting, and account management before a single deliverable ships. That's true whether you're at $4,000 or $20,000.  
>   
> Which means the math changes with scale:  
> 

| Monthly investment | Points | Running your account | Doing your work |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| $4,000 | 40 | 30% | 70% |
| $6,000 | 60 | 20% | 80% |
| $12,000 | 120 | 12% | 88% |

>   
> At smaller investments, a larger share funds coordination rather than production. That's not a markup — it's what it costs to run an account properly.  
>   
> It's also why we publish minimums. A discipline needs 20 to 25 points a month to run well. Below that, we'd be spending more on managing it than you'd get back in results. **We'd rather tell you that up front than take the money and under-deliver.**

*This section converts a weakness into credibility. Nobody else in the category shows this arithmetic, and it makes the tier structure self-evident rather than arbitrary.*

## Section 6 — What we don't do

> ## What we won't promise

> **We don't guarantee a number of leads.**  
>   
> We could. Plenty of agencies do. But a lead guarantee only holds if the agency controls what produces leads — and no agency does. Leads depend on your positioning, your sales follow-up, your close rate, and whether your market is ready to buy.  
>   
> What actually happens when an agency is contractually on the hook for a number they're not hitting: they broaden targeting, drop the gates, and reclassify anything with an email address. The number gets met. Your CRM fills with noise. Your sales team stops trusting marketing.  
>   
> **Here's what we do instead.** We build a forecast from your numbers — your conversion rate, your sales-accepted rate, your close rate — and show every assumption behind it. Then we're accountable for every input on our side of that chain. When something underperforms, we can tell you which link broke.  
>   
> **If you're comparing us to an agency that guarantees leads, ask them two questions:** what's the contractual definition of a lead, and what happens if they miss? The answer to the second one is usually "we keep working." That's not a guarantee. It's a promise of more of what didn't work.

*This is the strongest section on the page and the one most likely to get softened internally. Don't let it. It converts our biggest perceived weakness into a reason to trust us, and the two questions are genuinely useful to the buyer.*

## Section 7 — Timeline honesty

> ## How long this takes

> B2B buying takes nine to ten months from first exposure to closed deal, and roughly three-quarters of it happens before a buyer contacts anyone. Research from HockeyStack shows it now takes around 266 tracked touchpoints and nearly 2,900 impressions to close an average B2B deal — up about 20% from the prior year.  
>   
> Which means: the content we publish in month one reaches someone in month two. They research through month five. They raise their hand around month six.  
>   
> **We plan for a six-month ramp**, longer if your deal size is above $50,000 or if you're not yet known in your category.  
>   
> That doesn't mean six months of waiting. Traffic and engagement move in the first sixty days, and we'll show you. But lead volume moves when your buyers are ready — not when a contract starts.  
>   
> Any agency promising pipeline in 30 days is either selling to a market that buys impulsively, or they're not telling you the truth.

*Pair with the full benchmark document, "What It Actually Takes," in every proposal.*

## Section 8 — Technology

> ## Software costs are separate, and we show them

> Most engagements need tools — automation, enrichment, ad platforms, analytics. We itemize those separately from services rather than burying them in a monthly fee.  
>   
> Your portal shows every platform running on your account, what each costs, and whether we hold the contract or you do. Not just at signature — continuously, so you always know what's in your stack and what it's costing you.  
>   
> It means you can see what you're paying for software versus what you're paying for work, and you can make your own decisions about the stack. It also means your service fee buys service.

*This preempts the "why did my invoice change" conversation during migration, and it's genuinely differentiating — most agencies bury it.*

# Talk Tracks

**"That's more than we budgeted."**

> "Understood. Let's look at what fits. At \[their number\] you're at \[tier\], which is \[X\] program run properly. The question is whether that's the right one — and if the roadmap says you need more than that, I'd rather tell you now."

**"Can we start smaller and scale up?"**

> "Yes, and most clients do. The thing to know is that one program well-executed beats two run thin. If we split $5,000 across content and paid, neither gets enough to work. Pick the one that matters most, do it properly, and add the second when the budget's there."

**"What if we just want a few blog posts?"**

> "Then we're probably not the right fit, and I'd rather say that than sell you something. We're built for companies that want a team, not a freelancer. If you need production against a brief you're already running, there are cheaper ways to get it."

**"Why should I pay for strategy when I have a marketing lead?"**

> "You shouldn't. That's Execute — you own the plan, we execute against it, and you're not paying for a strategy layer you don't need. It's the cheaper option and for a lot of companies it's the right one."

**"Another agency quoted half this."**

> "They might be a better fit. Worth asking who's actually doing the work and where they sit — a lot of that pricing assumes offshore production or one generalist covering everything. Neither is wrong, but it's a different product. Ask them the same question you asked me: what does a typical month look like, and who does it?"

**"How do I know this is working?"**

> "In the first sixty days, traffic and engagement. By month three we should see conversion moving. Around month six, lead volume — that's when enough of your market has encountered you and reached their own buying moment. We'll set the baseline in month one so we're measuring movement, not arguing about whether a number is good."

**"Can you guarantee results?"**

> "No, and I'd be skeptical of anyone who does. What I can do is model what to expect from your numbers, show you every assumption, and commit to everything on our side of the chain. Then when something's off, we know which link broke instead of guessing."

# Language Rules

**Say:** team, points, program, service category, forecast, ramp, baseline **Don't say:** full-service, ROI-driven, results-oriented, growth partner, needle-moving

**Always name the roles.** "A writer, a designer, a developer, a marketing ops specialist, an analyst" beats "a full team" every time.

**Never apologize for the price.** The overhead math justifies it. Present it as arithmetic, not as a number to negotiate down from.

**Lead with what we won't do.** It's counterintuitive and it works. An agency that names its limits reads as more trustworthy than one that says yes to everything.

**Use their numbers, not ours.** Every forecast, every projection, every timeline should be derived from data they gave us. "Based on your 1.8% conversion rate" is unarguable in a way that "typically we see" is not.
